Key assumption. These estimates treat every ride as identical, as if crime were spread evenly across every hour, line and station in the system. That is obviously untrue, since crime clusters at particular stations, times and situations, so any one rider's real risk could be meaningfully higher or lower than what is shown here.

How to read this. Each figure applies the systemwide rate of reported crime per million rides to the number of rides you choose, as if every ride carried average risk. These counts include incidents involving transit workers and others who were not riding. Risk estimates use a Poisson model, so they never exceed 100% and account for the chance of more than one incident.

Any crime. This total counts every crime complaint recorded in the subway, including offenses that surface mainly through police enforcement, such as fare evasion, trespassing and weapons or drugs found during searches. Its rate reflects police activity as well as victimization, so it overstates a rider's chance of being harmed.

At this pace. The average number of years between incidents for someone riding this often. It depends only on how often you ride, so it stays the same whichever time frame you choose.

Presets. The slider runs to 750 rides a year, about two a day. The average divides about 1.28 billion annual subway rides by 8.585 million residents; tourists and commuters from outside the city inflate that figure somewhat. Commuter presets assume a round trip on each office day across 50 working weeks.

Source: NYPD transit crime complaints and MTA subway ridership; rates per million rides.
